<Callout type="problem">
Your manager asks for "100% uptime". Your cloud provider promises 99.99% for its load balancer and 99.95% for its database. Your own code has bugs and you deploy 10 times a day. **What availability can you realistically promise? And how do you decide when to slow down and fix reliability instead of shipping features?**
</Callout>

## Availability in “nines”

<Term k="availability">Availability</Term> = the fraction of time (or of requests) the system works correctly.

<NinesCalc />

| Availability | Name | Downtime per year | Per month |
|---|---|---|---|
| 99% | two nines | ~3.65 days | ~7.2 hours |
| 99.9% | three nines | ~8.8 hours | ~43 minutes |
| 99.95% | | ~4.4 hours | ~22 minutes |
| 99.99% | four nines | ~53 minutes | ~4.3 minutes |
| 99.999% | five nines | ~5.3 minutes | ~26 seconds |

Each extra nine is **10× less downtime** and usually much **more than 10× the cost**: redundancy everywhere, multiple regions, automated failover, careful deploys and on-call engineers.

## Availability math

- **In series** (A calls B calls C): multiply. 99.9% × 99.9% × 99.9% ≈ **99.7%**. Every dependency on the critical path lowers your availability.
- **In parallel** (two independent copies, either can serve): 1 − (1 − A)². Two 99% copies → **99.99%**. That's why redundancy works.

Practical consequences: reduce critical-path dependencies, make non-critical features **degrade gracefully** instead of failing the request, and add redundancy where it counts.

## SLI, SLO, SLA

<Term k="sla">These three acronyms</Term> come up a lot:

| Term | What it is | Example | Who cares |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SLI** (indicator) | A measurement | % of checkout requests that succeed in < 500 ms | Engineers |
| **SLO** (objective) | Your **internal target** for an SLI | 99.9% over 30 days | The team |
| **SLA** (agreement) | A **contract** with customers, with penalties (refunds, credits) | 99.5%, or we refund 10% | Business, legal |

The SLA is always **looser** than the SLO, so you get an internal warning before you owe anyone money.

## Error budgets: turning reliability into a decision

If the SLO is 99.9%, you're **allowed** 0.1% failure: about 43 minutes a month. That's the **error budget**.

- Budget left? → Ship features, take risks, deploy often.
- Budget burned? → Freeze risky launches; spend time on reliability work.

This turns "move fast" vs "be stable" from an argument into a number both product and engineering agree on. (This idea comes from Google's SRE practice.)

<Callout type="why" title="Why not aim for 100%?">
Users can't tell 99.999% from 100%, because their own Wi-Fi, phone and ISP fail far more often than that. Chasing 100% makes every change terrifyingly slow and expensive. Pick the target that matches what users actually need: a payment system needs more nines than an internal admin dashboard.
</Callout>

<Callout type="interview">
In the non-functional requirements, state a target: "**99.99% availability** for reading the feed, **99.9%** for posting." Later, justify your redundancy with it: "To reach four nines we need no single point of failure, multi-AZ deployment and automatic failover." Knowing that **3 nines ≈ 8.8 h/year** and **4 nines ≈ 53 min/year** off the top of your head is impressive.
</Callout>

<Takeaways>
- Each extra **nine** = 10× less downtime and much higher cost.
- **Series** components multiply availability (it gets worse); **parallel** copies improve it: 1 − (1 − A)².
- **SLI** = measurement, **SLO** = internal target, **SLA** = contract with penalties (looser than the SLO).
- **Error budgets** turn reliability into a shared decision rule.
</Takeaways>
