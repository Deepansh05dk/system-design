<Callout type="problem">
A fire, a flood, a power grid failure, or simply a cloud provider's bad configuration push, and an entire region goes offline for 8 hours. It has happened to every major cloud provider. Or worse: an engineer runs a script that deletes the production database. **Can your company survive this? How much data would you lose, and how long until you're back?**
</Callout>

## Two numbers define your plan

- **<Term k="rpo">RPO</Term> (Recovery Point Objective):** how much **data** can you afford to lose? "RPO = 1 hour" means losing the last hour of writes is acceptable.
- **RTO (Recovery Time Objective):** how long can you afford to be **down**? "RTO = 4 hours" means you must be back within 4 hours.

Smaller numbers = safer = more expensive. A bank's ledger might need RPO ≈ 0, RTO of minutes. An internal reporting tool might accept RPO 24 h, RTO 1 day.

## Failure domains

Cloud providers split the world into:
- **Regions** (e.g. Mumbai, Frankfurt): far apart, independent.
- **Availability zones (AZs)** inside a region: separate data centers with separate power and networking, a few km apart, connected by fast links.

Running across **multiple AZs** in one region protects against a single data center failing and is cheap and standard (most managed databases offer "multi-AZ" with synchronous replication). Surviving a **whole region** going down needs a **multi-region** plan.

## Backups: the last line of defense

Replication protects against machines dying, but **not** against bad writes: if someone runs `DELETE FROM orders` by mistake, replication faithfully deletes it everywhere within milliseconds. You also need:

- **Regular backups** + **point-in-time recovery** (continuous WAL archiving lets you restore to "10:41:59, just before the bad script").
- Backups stored in **another region/account**, ideally immutable (protection from ransomware or a compromised account).
- **Tested restores.** A backup you've never restored is a hope, not a backup. Run restore drills.
- Remember the 3-2-1 rule: 3 copies, 2 different media/services, 1 off-site.

## Multi-region active-active: the hard parts

Serving users from several regions at once gives the best latency and resilience, but:
- **Data replication across regions** is slow (~100+ ms), so writes are either slow (synchronous, strongly consistent) or conflicting (asynchronous, multi-leader).
- **Conflict resolution:** two regions update the same record at the same moment. Options: last-write-wins, CRDTs, or **home-region per user** (each user's writes go to one region, avoiding conflicts).
- **Routing:** GeoDNS / anycast send users to the nearest healthy region, and away from a failed one.
- **Cost:** you pay for (at least) double capacity, so each region can absorb the other's traffic.

<Callout type="analogy">
RPO is "how many pages of your notebook can you afford to lose?" RTO is "how long can you go without a notebook?" Photocopying every page instantly into a second notebook in another city (active-active) is safest and most expensive. Photocopying once a week and keeping it at home (backup & restore) is cheap, but you might lose a week and spend a day rebuilding.
</Callout>

<Callout type="interview">
For most designs, say: "Deployed across **3 availability zones**; DB with **synchronous standby** in another AZ and automatic failover; **daily snapshots + point-in-time recovery** in another region." For global, high-availability products (payments, messaging), discuss **multi-region** and the hard part: **cross-region data consistency** (e.g. home region per user, async replication with RPO of seconds).
</Callout>

<Takeaways>
- **RPO** = acceptable data loss; **RTO** = acceptable downtime. They drive the design and the cost.
- **Multi-AZ** protects against data center failures (standard); **multi-region** protects against region failures (costly).
- Strategies: **backup & restore → pilot light → warm standby → active-active** (more protection, more cost).
- Replication isn't backup: keep **point-in-time, off-site, tested** backups.
</Takeaways>
