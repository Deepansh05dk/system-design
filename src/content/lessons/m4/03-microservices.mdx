<Callout type="problem">
Your startup's app is one codebase. Now 80 engineers work in it. Every deploy needs 6 teams to coordinate; a bug in "recommendations" crashes "checkout"; the search feature needs 20× more servers than everything else, but you can only scale the whole app together. **Should you split it up? And what new problems does that create?**
</Callout>

## The two shapes

<Callout type="analogy">
A monolith is a **food truck**: one kitchen, one team, very efficient while small. Microservices are a **food court**: each stall has its own kitchen and staff, can expand independently, and one stall closing doesn't close the others, but now you need shared seating, a payment system, cleaners and a manager to coordinate.
</Callout>

## How to split: by business capability

Good service boundaries follow **business domains** (users, orders, payments, catalog, notifications), not technical layers ("the database service", "the validation service"). Each service:
- **owns its data**: its own database, which no other service touches directly,
- exposes an **API** (or events),
- can be built, deployed and scaled by **one team**.

<Callout type="mistake">
A "distributed monolith": services that share one database or must be deployed together. You get all the costs of microservices and none of the benefits. If two services always change together, they should probably be one service.
</Callout>

## Communication between services

- **Synchronous** (REST/gRPC): simple request-response, but creates **temporal coupling**: if payment is down, ordering fails. Chains of sync calls multiply latency and failure rates.
- **Asynchronous** (events/queues): "OrderPlaced" is published; others react when they can. More resilient and decoupled; eventually consistent.

Rule of thumb: **sync for queries that need an answer now; async for "something happened, react to it"**.

## What microservices require (the hidden cost)

Centralized logging, distributed tracing (Module 5), API gateway, service discovery, per-service CI/CD pipelines, container orchestration (Kubernetes), contract testing, on-call per team. That's why the advice is: **start with a well-structured (modular) monolith, and extract services when there's a real reason**: team scaling, wildly different scaling needs, or fault isolation.

<Callout type="interview">
In interviews, it's fine to draw services (User, Post, Feed, Notification…) since it clarifies responsibilities. But show judgment: "These are logical services; at our early stage they could live in one deployable, and we'd split out **Feed** first because its scaling profile is very different." Mention **each service owns its data**, **async events between services**, and **service discovery** via Kubernetes.
</Callout>

<Takeaways>
- **Monolith:** simple and fast to build; struggles with many teams and uneven scaling.
- **Microservices:** independent deploys and scaling, but network failures, distributed data and heavy infrastructure.
- Split by **business capability**; each service **owns its data**. Avoid the distributed monolith.
- **Service discovery** (registry / K8s DNS) finds healthy instances; service meshes handle retries/mTLS.
- **Start modular monolith**, extract services for clear reasons.
</Takeaways>
