<Callout type="problem">
Your database has copies in Mumbai and Frankfurt. The undersea cable between them is cut. A user in Mumbai updates their address; a moment later, a user in Frankfurt reads it. Frankfurt has no way to know about the update. **Should Frankfurt answer with possibly-old data, or refuse to answer at all?** There's no third option.
</Callout>

## The three letters

The <Term k="cap">CAP theorem</Term> (Eric Brewer, 2000) talks about three properties of a distributed data store:

- **C, Consistency:** every read sees the **latest** write (as if there were only one copy). *Note: this is not the "C" in ACID.*
- **A, Availability:** every request to a non-failed node gets a **response** (not an error), even if it's not the latest data.
- **P, Partition tolerance:** the system keeps working even when the network **splits** into groups that can't talk to each other.

The famous slogan is "pick 2 of 3". That's misleading. Here's the accurate version:

> In a distributed system, **network partitions will happen** (cables get cut, switches fail, cloud zones lose connectivity). So P is not optional. **During a partition, you must choose between C and A.**

When there's no partition, you can have both C and A.

<Callout type="analogy">
Two bank branches lose their phone line. A customer asks Branch B for their balance. **CP branch:** "Sorry, our system is down, come back later." **AP branch:** "Here's the balance as of this morning." Which is right depends on whether you're **withdrawing cash** (better be CP) or just **checking roughly** (AP is fine).
</Callout>

It's not all-or-nothing per system. A good design picks **per feature**: an e-commerce site can be AP for the product catalog and reviews, and CP for checkout and inventory.

## PACELC: what about when nothing is broken?

CAP only talks about partitions, which are rare. **PACELC** (Daniel Abadi) adds the everyday trade-off:

> **If P**artition → choose **A** or **C**. **E**lse (normal operation) → choose **L**atency or **C**onsistency.

Even with a perfect network, keeping copies strongly consistent means **waiting** for other replicas to confirm (sometimes across continents). So you trade latency for consistency **all the time**, not just during failures.

| System | During partition | Normally | Label |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cassandra / DynamoDB (default) | Availability | Latency | PA/EL |
| Spanner / CockroachDB | Consistency | Consistency | PC/EC |

Many databases are **tunable**: Cassandra lets you choose consistency per query (ONE, QUORUM, ALL), which moves you along this spectrum. Next lesson.

<Callout type="mistake">
"My system is CA." In a distributed system that's not a real option, because partitions happen whether you like it or not. A single-node database is "CA" only because it isn't distributed. In interviews, talk about **CP vs AP during partitions** and **latency vs consistency** normally.
</Callout>

<Callout type="interview">
Use CAP to justify choices per feature: "**Seat inventory must be CP.** If we can't confirm with the primary, we'd rather show an error than double-book. **The news feed is AP.** Showing a post a few seconds late is fine, and availability matters more." Interviewers love hearing the trade-off named explicitly.
</Callout>

<Takeaways>
- **CAP:** during a network **partition**, a distributed system must give up either **Consistency** or **Availability**.
- **P is not optional** in distributed systems, so the real choice is **CP vs AP** (when partitioned).
- **PACELC:** even without partitions, there's a constant **latency vs consistency** trade-off.
- Choose **per feature**: money/inventory → CP; feeds/likes/catalogs → AP.
</Takeaways>
